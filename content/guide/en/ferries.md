Sifnos has no airport, so unless you arrive by helicopter or private boat, you arrive by sea. That makes the ferry the single most important booking of your trip, and the one most likely to go wrong if you leave it late in August. This guide covers the routes, the realistic journey times, how the two types of vessel differ, and what actually happens when the summer wind gets up.

![Kamares](/images/sifnos/beaches/kamares.webp "The ferry bay at Kamares, the only port on Sifnos")

## Kamares: the island's only port

Every passenger ferry serving Sifnos docks at **Kamares**, a sheltered bay on the west coast. There is no second port and no alternative arrival point, which keeps things simple: whatever ticket you hold, you will step off at the same quay.

Kamares is a working port and a resort in its own right, with a long sandy beach, tavernas along the waterfront, a bakery, a small supermarket and a handful of hotels. If you land late at night, staying here rather than driving on is a perfectly good decision.

Apollonia, the island capital, sits about 5.5 km inland and uphill, roughly ten minutes by car.

## Piraeus to Sifnos: the main route

Piraeus is the port that serves Athens, about 45 to 60 minutes from Athens International Airport by metro, bus or taxi. Almost all Sifnos traffic runs through it.

Two very different kinds of vessel work the route:

| | Conventional ferry | High-speed catamaran |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical duration | About 5 hours | About 2.5 to 3 hours |
| Ride in rough sea | Steadier, heavier hull | Noticeably bouncier |
| Deck access | Open decks, walk around freely | Enclosed cabin, seated |
| Carries vehicles | Yes | Usually yes, limited space |
| Cancels in strong wind | Rarely | More often |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |

The conventional boat stops at Kythnos and Serifos on the way, which is why it takes longer. Many regulars actively prefer it: you get sea air, a proper seat, a coffee on deck, and a far better chance of sailing when the wind is up.

The high-speed saves you two hours or more. If you are connecting from an afternoon flight and want to be swimming the same evening, it is the obvious choice.

### Which should you book?

Take the **high-speed** if you are short on time, travelling light, and not prone to seasickness. Take the **conventional ferry** if you are bringing a vehicle, travelling with small children or older relatives, are sensitive to motion, or are travelling in late July and August when the meltemi is most likely to disrupt fast craft.

## The Western Cyclades line and island hopping

Sifnos sits on what is known as the Western Cyclades line. Depending on the season and the operator, sailings connect it with:

- **Kythnos** and **Serifos**, immediately to the north, both short hops
- **Milos** and **Kimolos**, to the south, also short
- **Folegandros**, **Sikinos**, **Ios** and **Santorini** on longer seasonal routings
- **Paros** and **Syros**, giving access to the Central Cyclades, on seasonal links

This makes Sifnos a genuinely good base for a two-island trip. Sifnos with Milos is the most popular pairing, and the crossing is short enough to do as a long day out, though staying a few nights is more rewarding.

Note that operators reshuffle these routes every year, and some connections only run in July and August. Always check the current season's timetable rather than relying on last year's pattern.

## Booking: when and how

**In July and August, book as far ahead as you can.** Weekend sailings and the popular late-afternoon departures sell out, and August around the 15th, a major public holiday in Greece, is the tightest period of the entire year.

A few things worth knowing:

- Check whether your ticket is **numbered seating** or open seating. On high-speed craft it is almost always assigned.
- If you are bringing your own car from Athens, book the vehicle space separately and early. Deck space is far more limited than passenger space.
- Most operators require you to collect a paper boarding pass or scan a code at the port. Allow 30 to 45 minutes before departure at Piraeus, which is a very large and confusing port.
- Piraeus has multiple gates spread over a long waterfront. Check your gate number on the ticket and allow time to walk.

## The meltemi and cancelled sailings

The **meltemi** is a strong, dry northerly wind that blows across the Aegean mainly from mid-July through August. It is not a storm, it is a feature of the season, and it can reach a strength that stops fast ferries from sailing.

What this means in practice:

- High-speed catamarans are suspended at a lower wind threshold than conventional ferries.
- Cancellations are usually announced the evening before or the morning of departure.
- If your sailing is cancelled you are entitled to a refund or a transfer to another sailing, but seats fill fast.

**The practical lesson:** do not schedule your return ferry for the same day as an international flight home. Leave a night in Athens as a buffer. Travellers who ignore this are the ones you see sprinting through Piraeus.

## Winter and shoulder season

From November to March the timetable thins to roughly three or four sailings a week, mostly conventional ferries, and bad weather can cut that further. Sifnos stays inhabited and open year round, but a winter visit needs flexible plans.

April, May, September and October are the sweet spot: daily or near daily sailings, calm seas, warm water in September, and none of the August scramble.
